# The Brain Changes Consciousness. Does It Create It?

## What neuroscience can show—and what it still cannot—about whether the brain generates, realizes, filters, or expresses conscious experience.

> Brain injury, stimulation, drugs, sleep, anesthesia, and disease can alter consciousness with extraordinary precision. That establishes deep dependence. It does not, by itself, settle every metaphysical question about what consciousness ultimately is.

**An EmberFire Media publication.**  
*Research reviewed through September 23, 2026.*

There is a sentence that can end a consciousness argument before it begins:

**Change the brain and you change the mind.**

Damage the visual cortex and vision can disappear or fragment. Stimulate particular networks and perception can change. Alter receptor systems and the sense of self, time, memory, pain, and reality can shift. General anesthetics can turn a responsive person into someone who neither moves nor later reports experience. Sleep can disconnect us from the environment while still producing dreams. Severe brain injury can leave a patient behaviorally unresponsive even when brain imaging reveals command-following.

The dependence is real.

What is less obvious is what kind of conclusion follows from it.

Does the brain **produce** consciousness the way a liver produces bile? Does it **realize** consciousness in the sense that conscious states simply are certain organized brain states? Does it **constrain or filter** something more fundamental? Is experience itself a basic feature of reality? Or are some of the properties we attribute to consciousness artifacts of the way cognition represents itself?

Those are not interchangeable questions.

The scientific problem becomes especially difficult because several theories can explain the same observation after the fact. If every possible result can be absorbed by every model, accumulating more mysteries does not move the argument forward.

The useful question is therefore not:

**Which theory sounds most satisfying?**

It is:

**What observation would make rival theories predict different things?**

## First, consciousness is not one measurable thing

Researchers use the word consciousness for several related but separable phenomena.

There is **arousal**: whether an organism is awake or capable of waking.

There is **phenomenal consciousness**: whether there is something it feels like to undergo the state.

There is **access consciousness**: whether information is available for reasoning, decision-making, report, or behavioral control.

There is **self-consciousness**: awareness of oneself as the subject of experience.

There is **metacognition**: the ability to evaluate one's own cognitive states.

And there is the distinction between a **global state**—waking, dreaming, anesthesia, coma—and a particular **content** within that state, such as seeing red, feeling pain, remembering a face, or experiencing a loss of self-boundaries.

A major review by Anil Seth and Tim Bayne emphasized that contemporary consciousness science contains multiple active theoretical frameworks rather than one accepted mechanism: higher-order approaches, global-workspace theories, re-entry and predictive-processing approaches, and Integrated Information Theory among them.<sup><a href="#note-1">1</a></sup>

That lack of consensus does not mean neuroscience has learned nothing.

It means the field has become precise enough to discover that the word consciousness was hiding several problems at once.

## Brain dependence is not a weak result

Any serious theory has to begin with a fact that is difficult to explain away:

**Specific changes to the brain produce specific, repeatable changes in conscious life.**

This is stronger than a vague correlation.

Lesions can selectively disrupt visual awareness, memory, language, face recognition, spatial awareness, bodily ownership, emotion, or the capacity to form new memories. Electrical or magnetic stimulation can alter perception and behavior. Anesthetics act on molecular targets and neural circuits and can profoundly change arousal, responsiveness, memory formation, and large-scale network dynamics. Disorders of consciousness track major changes in brain function even when behavioral examination is imperfect.<sup><a href="#note-2">2</a></sup>

The precision matters.

If damage to one system reliably removes one capacity while leaving others relatively intact, a model in which the brain merely receives an undifferentiated external consciousness now has work to do. It must explain why the proposed receiver has such fine-grained mapping between local neural organization and particular contents of experience.

This distinction is often abused in both directions.

One side says: because brain dependence is overwhelming, the metaphysical problem is already solved.

The other says: because dependence does not logically prove production, brain evidence is almost irrelevant.

Neither follows.

The correct position is more demanding.

Brain dependence is a massive empirical constraint. A non-production model has to explain it at least as well as a production model does.

## The 2025 theory test did something consciousness research badly needed

For years, major theories of consciousness accumulated evidence largely in parallel.

Then a large open-science collaboration directly tested preregistered predictions derived from Integrated Information Theory and Global Neuronal Workspace Theory. Published in Nature in 2025, the project was designed as an adversarial collaboration: supporters of rival frameworks helped specify in advance what evidence should matter.<sup><a href="#note-3">3</a></sup>

The result was not a clean coronation.

Some findings fit parts of each framework. Other findings challenged predictions associated with each. The broader importance is methodological.

A consciousness theory becomes scientifically useful when it risks being wrong.

A result that every theory can reinterpret is background evidence.

A result that different theories predicted differently is leverage.

That may be the most important change in modern consciousness research.

## A person can be behaviorally silent without being cognitively absent

The bedside examination creates a dangerous temptation: if a patient does not respond, assume there is no meaningful cognition.

A 2024 multicenter study in the New England Journal of Medicine tested 353 adults with disorders of consciousness using standardized behavioral assessment plus task-based fMRI, EEG, or both. Among 241 participants who showed no observable command-following, 60—25 percent—showed command-following detectable in brain activity.<sup><a href="#note-4">4</a></sup>

This phenomenon is called **cognitive motor dissociation**.

It is one of the clearest demonstrations that behavior, access, motor output, and cognition can come apart.

But it is also routinely overinterpreted.

The study does **not** show consciousness without a functioning brain. The evidence of hidden command-following was measured through brain activity.

What it shows is that our ordinary behavioral window can fail.

That distinction matters enormously for coma diagnosis, medical ethics, prognosis, and the logic of consciousness research.

A person may be more cognitively present than outward behavior suggests.

That is remarkable.

It is not disembodied consciousness.

## Anesthesia is a controlled assault on simplistic theories

General anesthesia is especially useful because it allows researchers to manipulate conscious state repeatedly and reversibly.

Modern reviews emphasize that anesthetics alter molecular targets, arousal systems, sensory processing, cortical and subcortical circuits, and large-scale functional networks.<sup><a href="#note-5">5</a></sup>

But anesthesia also exposes a conceptual trap.

**Unresponsiveness is not automatically the same as absence of experience.**

Some anesthetic states may block responsiveness, memory formation, environmental access, or report more strongly than they block every possible form of experience. This is why researchers distinguish consciousness, connectedness to the environment, responsiveness, and later recall.

Sleep provides a parallel lesson.

A sleeping person may be disconnected from the room, unable to provide an immediate report, and later remember nothing—yet dreaming demonstrates that vivid conscious experience can occur during sleep. Contemporary sleep research uses differences between dreaming and dreamless periods as another way to isolate neural conditions associated with experience.<sup><a href="#note-6">6</a></sup>

The scientific target is therefore not merely:

**Is the person moving?**

It is:

**What capacities remain, which neural organizations remain, and what kind of evidence could distinguish absent experience from absent access, memory, or output?**

## Psychedelics do not give us a brain that has switched off

Psychedelic states are sometimes presented as evidence that reducing normal brain control releases a consciousness that exists independently of the brain.

There is a real observation underneath that claim.

A 2024 Nature study repeatedly scanned healthy participants before and after psilocybin and found major disruption of functional connectivity across cortex and subcortex. The changes were especially prominent in systems associated with the sense of self, space, and time. The authors described broad desynchronization of functional brain organization, with some connectivity changes persisting beyond the acute state.<sup><a href="#note-7">7</a></sup>

Subjectively, the state can become extraordinarily rich while ordinary network organization becomes less stable.

That is interesting.

But **less synchronized is not less active**, and disrupted network boundaries are not equivalent to a silent brain.

The psychedelic state therefore challenges crude equations such as “more ordinary organization equals more consciousness.”

It does not establish that consciousness left the brain.

The stronger lesson is that the relationship between neural organization and experiential richness is nonlinear.

Some forms of constraint may support ordinary selfhood and task performance while limiting other forms of experience.

That finding is compatible with several theories.

To discriminate among them, a filter model would have to predict something beyond the already-known fact that changing neural constraints changes experience.

## Cardiac arrest is where timing becomes everything

Near-death experiences sit at the most emotionally and scientifically charged edge of this debate.

Reports can include vivid perception, unusual clarity, a sense of leaving the body, encounters with beings or deceased relatives, life review, altered time, or a powerful sense of reality.

The experiences themselves are real as reported experiences.

The difficult question is **when they occurred** and what physiological state existed at that time.

The AWARE II study, published in Resuscitation in 2023, prospectively examined 567 in-hospital cardiac arrests. Fifty-three people survived to hospital discharge; 28 completed interviews; 11 reported memories or perceptions categorized as suggestive of consciousness.<sup><a href="#note-8">8</a></sup>

The study also attempted objective audiovisual testing.

Nobody identified the hidden visual image. One of 28 interviewees identified the auditory stimulus. The investigators also reported organized EEG activity—including delta, theta, and alpha patterns—emerging during prolonged CPR in some recordings, even after substantial ischemia.

Those details matter because the popular phrase **“consciousness after the brain shut down”** compresses several different claims into one.

Cardiac arrest severely disrupts circulation and brain function.

It does not automatically provide continuous measurement proving that every relevant neural process was absent during every moment later represented in memory.

A scalp EEG is also not a meter for all activity everywhere in the brain. Electrode coverage, noise during CPR, sensitivity to deep structures, drugs, temperature, cerebral blood flow, and timing all matter.

A decisive survival-of-consciousness experiment would require more.

It would need a verifiable perception whose information could not plausibly have been acquired through ordinary channels, linked to a period with dense physiological recording that sharply constrains when the information was acquired.

That is a much higher bar than “the patient later reported a vivid experience.”

It is also the kind of bar an extraordinary claim should welcome.

## Lucid episodes in severe dementia are real enough to study—and not yet understood

Accounts of a person with severe dementia unexpectedly speaking coherently shortly before death have circulated for generations.

For a long time, the literature was dominated by retrospective caregiver reports.

That is changing.

A prospective observational study published in 2025 followed 20 people living with advanced dementia using longitudinal audiovisual observations. Across 539 observations, investigators validated nine lucid episodes in three participants. Increased meaningful vocalization was a shared feature, although the episodes varied across individuals.<sup><a href="#note-9">9</a></sup>

This moves the subject from folklore toward measurable clinical observation.

It does not tell us the mechanism.

A temporary return of coherent communication could reflect fluctuating network function, preserved capacities that ordinary testing misses, changes in arousal, transient compensation, or mechanisms not yet characterized.

It does not automatically show that memories are stored outside the brain.

The scientifically interesting position is not to dismiss the event or inflate it.

It is to record it prospectively, time-lock it, measure physiology before and during it, and ask what changed.

## The filter theory is older and more serious than the modern receiver metaphor

The idea that the brain might transmit or restrict consciousness rather than create it did not begin with internet analogies about radios.

William James discussed a transmission-type possibility in Human Immortality in 1898. Henri Bergson developed a different but related account in Matter and Memory, arguing against treating memory and consciousness as straightforward products mechanically stored in brain tissue.<sup><a href="#note-10">10</a></sup>

Historically, these are genuine philosophical positions.

Scientifically, however, a metaphor is not yet a mechanism.

Calling the brain a receiver raises immediate questions:

Receiver of what?

By what physical or informational process?

Why do local lesions cause such specific losses?

Why do anesthetic molecules produce reproducible state changes?

How is personal identity bound to one organism?

How is information transferred?

What observation would occur if transmission theory were true that should not occur under a sufficiently detailed production model?

Without answers, “filter” risks becoming a label attached to any unexplained case.

A viable filter theory has to risk failure.

## Panpsychism does not mean rocks think like people

Another family of theories changes the starting point rather than proposing a signal entering the brain.

Panpsychist approaches hold, in very broad terms, that mentality or experience-related properties are fundamental and widespread rather than appearing from wholly non-experiential reality only after brains become complex.

Neutral monism proposes that the underlying basis of reality is neither intrinsically mental nor intrinsically physical, with the mental and physical understood as derivative descriptions or aspects.<sup><a href="#note-11">11</a></sup>

These views are philosophically active because they attempt to address the explanatory gap directly.

But a philosophical solution and an empirical confirmation are not the same thing.

Neuroscience failing to possess a complete theory of consciousness does not, by itself, confirm panpsychism.

Likewise, demonstrating ever tighter brain-consciousness relationships does not automatically answer why any physical processing is accompanied by experience at all.

This is where the **hard problem** and the **empirical problem** must be kept separate.

One concerns mechanisms, functions, contents, and causal dependencies.

The other concerns why those processes should have a subjective aspect in the first place.

Progress on one does not erase the other.

## Quantum consciousness is not one theory, and quantum biology is not enough

Quantum mechanics is unavoidable in the physics underlying biology.

That fact does not establish a quantum theory of consciousness.

A consciousness proposal has to specify the relevant quantum process, its biological substrate, how long coherence is maintained, how it influences neural computation, and what prediction differs from a classical neural model.

Max Tegmark's 2000 analysis argued that decoherence times for proposed neural quantum processes would be far shorter than the timescales relevant to cognition, favoring a classical treatment of those neural degrees of freedom.<sup><a href="#note-12">12</a></sup>

That analysis was challenged by Hameroff and colleagues, who argued that assumptions about the physical substrate and separation scales did not match their proposed microtubule model. The debate did not disappear.

In 2025, Michael Wiest argued in Neuroscience of Consciousness that experimental findings involving anesthetic action and microtubules support a quantum-microtubule substrate.<sup><a href="#note-13">13</a></sup>

That paper is evidence that the research program remains active.

It is not the same as field-wide validation.

The correct unit of analysis is not “quantum consciousness” as one package.

It is each proposal, one by one:

**substrate → coherence → biological coupling → distinctive prediction → replication.**

Anything less allows the word quantum to do explanatory work that the experiment has not done.

## Ancient traditions preserve observations of mind—not modern neuroscience in disguise

Human beings studied changes in dream, self, death, memory, and awareness long before laboratories existed.

That history matters.

But ancient categories should not be translated automatically into modern scientific ones.

The Greek word psychē changed substantially across centuries and philosophical schools. Plato, Aristotle, Epicureans, and Stoics did not share one theory of a detachable modern “consciousness.” Some traditions treated soul as responsible for life broadly; others developed more explicitly psychological accounts; some Hellenistic schools treated soul as corporeal.<sup><a href="#note-14">14</a></sup>

South Asian traditions developed detailed analyses of waking, dreaming, deep sleep, selfhood, attention, and contemplative experience—but Hindu, Buddhist, and later philosophical schools cannot responsibly be flattened into one “Eastern consciousness theory.”

Jewish visionary and mystical traditions likewise contain ascent, transformed perception, divine presence, and hidden knowledge. Their textual history belongs beside, not inside, modern neuroscience. For that development, see [Before the Tree of Life](/research/jewish-mysticism-kabbalah-history-sources).

Ancient testimony can establish that people described and systematized unusual states.

It cannot be treated as a laboratory measurement merely because a modern translation sounds familiar.

The history becomes stronger when we stop forcing it to predict twenty-first-century physics.

## The real contest is over predictions

A model can survive indefinitely if every anomaly is counted as evidence for it and every ordinary result is declared compatible with it.

That is not a test.

The useful research program asks rival models to commit in advance.

<div className="evidence-ladder">
  <div className="evidence-tier"><strong>LEVEL 1</strong><span><b>Correlation:</b> a brain pattern accompanies a reported experience.</span></div>
  <div className="evidence-tier"><strong>LEVEL 2</strong><span><b>Intervention:</b> manipulating a process changes a predicted aspect of experience.</span></div>
  <div className="evidence-tier"><strong>LEVEL 3</strong><span><b>Individual prediction:</b> the model predicts a person's state or content before the answer is known.</span></div>
  <div className="evidence-tier"><strong>LEVEL 4</strong><span><b>Rival separation:</b> competing theories predict different outcomes from the same preregistered test.</span></div>
  <div className="evidence-tier"><strong>LEVEL 5</strong><span><b>Independent replication:</b> the discriminating result survives new laboratories, methods, and adversarial scrutiny.</span></div>
</div>

For a production or realization theory, the challenge is to move from ever-better correlation and intervention toward an explanation of why those processes constitute experience.

For a filter theory, the challenge is to produce a unique, testable effect that cannot simply be redescribed as hidden neural processing.

For fundamental-consciousness theories, the challenge is to connect metaphysical claims to measurable organization without pretending that philosophical coherence is experimental proof.

For quantum proposals, the challenge is to demonstrate the specific quantum process in the relevant biological substrate and show that it matters causally for consciousness.

For survival claims, the challenge is time-locked information acquisition under conditions that exclude ordinary sensory and neural explanations.

The standards differ because the claims differ.

## What the evidence supports

The current record supports several conclusions strongly.

**The brain is deeply and causally involved in ordinary human consciousness.** Lesions, stimulation, anesthesia, sleep-state changes, pharmacology, development, and disease place powerful constraints on any theory.

**Behavior is an imperfect proxy for consciousness and cognition.** Covert command-following shows that a silent body can hide more brain-based cognitive capacity than bedside behavior reveals.

**Conscious experience does not map simply onto “more brain activity.”** Dreaming and psychedelics show that organization, connectivity, content, access, memory, and arousal matter more than a single activity dial.

**Near-death experiences and lucid episodes deserve research rather than dismissal.** Prospective work has documented phenomena worth explaining.

**Those phenomena do not currently establish consciousness operating independently of the brain.** Timing, measurement coverage, information source, and alternative neural mechanisms remain unresolved.

**No single neuroscientific theory of consciousness presently commands consensus.** Direct theory testing is improving, but the field is still active.

**Filter, panpsychist, neutral-monist, dualist, idealist, and quantum proposals cannot be rejected merely because they are nonstandard—but logical possibility is not positive evidence.** Each needs a mechanism or discriminating prediction proportionate to its claims.

And the most important conclusion is methodological:

**Unexplained is not the same thing as supernatural.**

But it is also not the same thing as unimportant.

The edge cases are valuable precisely because they force every theory to say what it would expect before the result arrives.

## Evidence status

- Precise brain dependence of many conscious capacities: **Strongly supported**
- Multiple competing scientific theories of consciousness: **Documented**
- 2025 adversarial IIT/GNWT study as a decisive winner-take-all result: **Not supported**
- Covert command-following in some behaviorally unresponsive patients: **Verified empirical finding**
- Cognitive motor dissociation as evidence of consciousness without brain activity: **Unsupported**
- Psychedelic experiential richness with major changes in brain-network organization: **Verified empirical domain**
- Psychedelic desynchronization as proof that the brain is only a filter: **Unsupported**
- Recalled experiences associated with cardiac arrest: **Documented**
- AWARE II proof of perception during demonstrated total absence of brain activity: **Not established**
- Prospective lucid episodes in advanced dementia: **Documented in a small observational sample**
- Filter/transmission theory as a historically genuine philosophical model: **Verified**
- Filter/transmission theory as an established scientific mechanism: **Speculative / underdetermined**
- Panpsychism and neutral monism as active philosophical positions: **Verified**
- Quantum microtubule consciousness as established neuroscience: **Disputed**
- Ancient traditions as evidence of long-standing inquiry into mind and altered states: **Verified historically**
- Ancient traditions as experimental confirmation of modern neuroscience or quantum theory: **Unsupported**
