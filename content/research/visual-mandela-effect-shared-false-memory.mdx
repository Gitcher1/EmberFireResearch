# The Memory Everyone Gets Wrong Together

## The Visual Mandela Effect survived replication—and a statistical correction. What it proves is stranger, and narrower, than either skeptics or alternate-reality theories usually admit.

> Shared false memories are real enough to replicate. The harder question is why different people can converge on the same wrong detail—and how to tell a memory error from a real historical variation before the internet contaminates the evidence.

**An EmberFire Media publication.**  
*Research current through September 19, 2026.*

There is no monocle on the canonical Monopoly mascot.

For many people, that sentence does not merely sound surprising. It feels visually wrong. The remembered figure arrives almost automatically: top hat, mustache, cane—and a monocle that the familiar image does not contain.

The same kind of conflict appears around Pikachu's tail, Curious George, C-3PO, the Volkswagen emblem, the Fruit of the Loom logo, the spelling of Berenstain, remembered film lines, product names, geography, anatomy, celebrity deaths, and historical events. The examples are often thrown into one enormous internet category called the **Mandela Effect**.

That category hides the most important fact.

These are not all the same kind of claim.

A laboratory result showing that people choose the same wrong version of a logo cannot prove that an old package design never existed. A trademark file cannot tell us what a child saw on a counterfeit shirt. A family archive can settle a spelling history without explaining why the alternative spelling feels so familiar. A theory from quantum mechanics does not automatically supply a mechanism by which a person could cross branches of reality while carrying only selected memories.

Once those problems are separated, the Mandela Effect becomes both less sensational and more interesting.

A controlled visual-memory effect has now survived independent replication. The replication also found that the original paper used the wrong statistical test for part of its analysis. The corrected result still preserved the central pattern. In parallel, archival cases show that some supposed “residue” is weak, some is derivative, some reflects ordinary historical variation, and some remains worth investigating because the primary record has not been audited deeply enough.

The evidence therefore does not support the two positions that dominate online arguments:

**“It is all just people remembering badly.”**

and

**“The fact that people remember the same thing proves reality changed.”**

The first is too vague to explain a replicated pattern of specific convergence. The second leaps far beyond what any experiment has measured.

The real question is narrower and harder:

**Why can many people reconstruct the same detail that is absent from the canonical record—and what kind of evidence could ever distinguish that process from a genuine alteration of the record itself?**

## One name, several different problems

The phrase *Mandela Effect* is generally attributed to writer Fiona Broome, who described a conversation at Dragon Con in 2009 in which she and others discussed a memory that Nelson Mandela had died in prison during the 1980s. Broome later built a website around similar reports and speculative explanations.<sup><a href="#note-1" aria-label="Research note 1">1</a></sup>

Even the origin story needs source criticism. Broome's account is the central first-person source for the 2009 conversation, but surviving contemporaneous web documentation is thinner than the later retellings suggest. Merriam-Webster currently records 2012 as the first known use it can document for the term.<sup><a href="#note-2" aria-label="Research note 2">2</a></sup> That does not prove Broome's 2009 account is wrong. It means the wording should remain precise: **the origin is creator-reported as 2009; independently indexed surviving usage is easier to establish later.**

More importantly, the label now contains several evidence problems that require different methods.

**Event memories** include Mandela dying in prison or other remembered public events that conflict with the surviving historical record.

**Visual-icon memories** include the Monopoly monocle, Fruit of the Loom cornucopia, Pikachu tail marking, Curious George's tail, C-3PO coloration, and logo geometry.

**Spelling and wording memories** include Berenstein/Berenstain, product names, slogans, and movie quotations.

**Media-existence claims** concern remembered films, scenes, broadcasts, advertisements, packaging, or editions that people cannot locate in current archives.

**Geographical, anatomical, and factual memories** concern maps, borders, body structures, dates, or other learned information.

A recognition experiment is suited to one of those classes. An archive is suited to another. A broadcast log, production master, trademark specimen, or authenticated physical object may be necessary for a third.

Treating all of them as one thing is the first methodological mistake.

## The original Mandela case: the history is clear; the memory source is not

The historical record for Nelson Mandela is unusually strong.

Mandela was imprisoned for 27 years, released from Victor Verster Prison on February 11, 1990, voted in South Africa's first fully democratic national election in April 1994, was inaugurated as president on May 10, 1994, and died at his Johannesburg home on December 5, 2013.<sup><a href="#note-3" aria-label="Research note 3">3</a></sup>

Those facts do not rest on one modern biography. They are embedded in an enormous contemporaneous record: news broadcasts, photographs, speeches, diplomatic visits, election materials, government records, Nobel documentation, and years of public appearances after the date on which the remembered prison death supposedly occurred.

That makes the claim that Mandela **actually died in prison in the 1980s within the historical record we possess** incompatible with the evidence.

It does not answer the psychologically interesting part.

Broome described remembering not merely a vague death but elements of public mourning and funeral coverage. Other people later reported similar recollections. What produced those details?

The source has not been identified.

Confusion with another anti-apartheid figure, recombination of different news stories, fictional material, later reconstruction, repeated discussion, or some combination of those processes are plausible possibilities. None should be promoted to a demonstrated explanation until a specific transmission path is found.

The case has another weakness that matters scientifically: the memories were collected retrospectively after the discrepancy had already become the subject of discussion. Once someone has been told that “other people remember Mandela dying,” that statement itself becomes part of the person's exposure history. Reports gathered afterward cannot be treated as though they were sealed, independent memories recorded before social contact.

The original Mandela story is therefore historically contradicted but **psychologically unresolved at the level of the memory's source**.

That distinction sets the standard for the rest of the investigation.

## The experiment that made the phenomenon testable

The strongest controlled evidence does not come from celebrity deaths. It comes from pictures.

In 2022, psychologists Deepasri Prasad and Wilma A. Bainbridge published *The Visual Mandela Effect as Evidence for Shared and Specific False Memories Across People* in *Psychological Science*.<sup><a href="#note-4" aria-label="Research note 4">4</a></sup>

Their first experiment gave 100 adults 40 familiar logos or characters. For each target, participants saw three versions: the canonical image and two manipulated alternatives. They chose the version they believed was correct and rated confidence, familiarity, and exposure.

The interesting result was not merely that participants made mistakes.

People make mistakes about visual details constantly.

The unusual result was that for certain targets, errors **converged on one particular wrong alternative**. Instead of a random spray of guesses, a specific false version could attract a substantial share of participants—even when the participants reported high familiarity with the object.

Seven images met the original paper's Visual Mandela Effect criteria: C-3PO, Curious George, Fruit of the Loom, Mr. Monopoly, Pikachu, Volkswagen, and Waldo.

The researchers then tested several obvious explanations.

A cursor-guided viewing experiment did not reveal one simple attentional pattern capable of accounting for the effect. An analysis of naturally occurring images did not show that the false variants simply dominated the visual environment. A free-drawing experiment showed that characteristic errors could also appear when participants generated an image from memory rather than selecting one of the experimenter's alternatives.<sup><a href="#note-4" aria-label="Research note 4">4</a></sup>

That last result matters. If people only chose the false version after seeing it in a multiple-choice display, the experiment itself could be manufacturing the error. Spontaneous drawing does not eliminate cultural exposure, but it shows that at least some of the characteristic distortions can be produced without first presenting the participant with the exact fake.

The paper turned an internet curiosity into an empirical question.

Then a replication found a problem.

## The replication corrected the statistics—and the effect remained

In 2024, Isaac Handley-Miner and Amanda Metskas conducted a preregistered replication of the main recognition study using 389 participants.<sup><a href="#note-5" aria-label="Research note 5">5</a></sup>

The replication is more informative than a simple “confirmed” or “failed” label because it did two things at once.

First, it reproduced the central phenomenon. All seven images highlighted by the original paper showed the expected pattern in the new sample, and an additional image—Tom from *Tom and Jerry*—also met the replication criteria.

Second, the replication team found analytical problems in the original work.

The 2022 paper had used a chi-square test of independence for a three-choice comparison in which the response categories were mutually exclusive. Choosing one image necessarily meant not choosing the others. The replication team concluded that a chi-square goodness-of-fit test was the appropriate analysis.

When they reanalyzed the original dataset using the corrected method, six of the seven original Visual Mandela images remained statistically significant. Waldo dropped below the conventional threshold in the corrected original sample, but Waldo was significant in the larger replication sample.<sup><a href="#note-5" aria-label="Research note 5">5</a></sup>

The team also argued that a split-half analysis in the original paper had been misinterpreted and reported that one exposure-related analysis could not be reproduced from the available description.

This is exactly the kind of result that gets flattened in public debate.

One retelling says: **the statistics were wrong, therefore the Mandela Effect was debunked.**

Another says: **the effect replicated, therefore the statistical criticism does not matter.**

Both erase information.

The original analysis contained a real mistake. Correcting it changed one original image-level result and reduced confidence in some secondary claims.

The independent replication nevertheless recovered the central pattern.

The replicated claim is therefore not that every famous Mandela example is genuine, nor that a single mechanism explains them all.

It is narrower:

> **Some familiar visual objects produce stable error attractors: independent groups of people can converge on the same noncanonical detail with surprising consistency and confidence.**

That is more than anecdote.

It is much less than evidence that physical history changed.

## Why the same wrong detail can feel right

The broader memory literature gives several reasons to expect systematic errors rather than pure randomness.

People often preserve **gist, category, meaning, and relational structure** better than exact surface detail. Classic research on memory for common objects showed that repeated exposure does not guarantee precise recall of their features.<sup><a href="#note-6" aria-label="Research note 6">6</a></sup> Research on gist-based false recognition similarly shows how related material can support a feeling of familiarity for details that were not actually presented.<sup><a href="#note-7" aria-label="Research note 7">7</a></sup>

Schemas matter too. A monocle fits the cultural schema of an old-fashioned wealthy gentleman. A black tail tip may feel compatible with other black markings on Pikachu. A logo may be reconstructed toward symmetry, semantic completeness, or the visual grammar of similar brands.

Source monitoring offers another pathway. A person can remember a feature but lose track of where it came from. The source might have been parody art, an imitation product, a costume, a meme, a knockoff, a neighboring character, a misprint, or another person's description.

Social memory can also alter retrieval. Research on memory in groups has long shown that information can be transmitted between people, though the effects are not uniform across every kind of distortion.<sup><a href="#note-8" aria-label="Research note 8">8</a></sup> A 2026 study of group discussion found that discussion could increase some distortions, including misattribution and persistence, while not increasing every measured “memory sin.”<sup><a href="#note-9" aria-label="Research note 9">9</a></sup>

That complexity matters. “Social contagion” should not become another universal explanation.

No single ordinary mechanism has yet been demonstrated to explain every Visual Mandela Effect item.

But “we do not yet have one mechanism” is not the same as “ordinary mechanisms have failed.”

It means the next experiments should predict the false detail in advance.

If schema fit, symmetry, source confusion, memorability, or feature redundancy are doing the work, researchers should be able to identify **new** images likely to generate a particular error before participants are tested. Prediction is stronger than explaining the result after it appears.

## The archive problem: memory experiments cannot settle historical variation

The laboratory work establishes that shared visual false recognition exists.

It does **not** establish that every disputed logo, package, quote, film scene, or object has always existed in only one form.

Real media change.

Logos are redesigned. Packaging is regional. Broadcasters edit films. Home-video releases differ. Licensed products are simplified. Printers make errors. Counterfeit merchandise circulates. School materials copy misspellings. Local advertisements use unofficial artwork. Parodies become more familiar than originals.

That means a specific Mandela claim should often begin with a different question:

**What versions actually existed, where, and when?**

Only after building that record can we decide whether a remembered form is absent from it.

This is why “I found residue” and “the archive proves it never happened” can both be premature.

The right method is **archive first, memory second**.

## Fruit of the Loom: the best example of why provenance matters

The Fruit of the Loom cornucopia is unusually valuable because it combines a replicated memory effect with a disputed archival claim.

Many people report remembering the fruit cluster sitting in front of or emerging from a brown cornucopia. The 2022 Visual Mandela Effect experiment found strong selection of a cornucopia-bearing false alternative.<sup><a href="#note-4" aria-label="Research note 4">4</a></sup>

Online discussions then point to an apparently explosive piece of documentary “residue”: a historical U.S. trademark design-code entry associated with Fruit of the Loom that includes language such as “baskets of fruit,” “containers of fruit,” and “cornucopia.”

That sounds, at first glance, like an official admission that a cornucopia existed.

It is not that simple.

Fruit of the Loom states that the wording came from an examiner-assigned search/design classification rather than from the actual image claimed as the mark, and the company denies that its official logo ever used a cornucopia.<sup><a href="#note-10" aria-label="Research note 10">10</a></sup> Current USPTO systems confirm longstanding Fruit of the Loom registrations and provide the route for inspecting registration histories and specimens.<sup><a href="#note-11" aria-label="Research note 11">11</a></sup>

A company statement is not independent evidence merely because the company is official. But neither is a cropped database screenshot proof of an image that the underlying filing may not contain.

The correct next step is not argument by screenshot.

It is a **complete primary-file audit**:

- original applications;
- registration certificates;
- examiner coding history;
- submitted specimens;
- dated company catalogs;
- newspaper advertisements;
- packaging;
- authenticated garments;
- regional licensing records;
- known counterfeits;
- parody artwork;
- and the earliest recoverable online appearance of the cornucopia claim.

Every object should carry provenance: where it came from, who authenticated it, when it was produced, and whether it was official, licensed, unofficial, or unknown.

If an authenticated company specimen containing a cornucopia surfaced, the historical claim would change immediately.

If hundreds of derivative cornucopia objects surfaced but none could be traced to an authorized source, that would support a different pathway: a culturally successful noncanonical variant.

Both outcomes are more informative than another poll.

## Berenstain: a case where the error has its own history

The Berenstain/Berenstein dispute illustrates a different mechanism.

The canonical family and publishing name is Berenstain. The family's own history says the unusual spelling was repeatedly normalized by other people long before the modern Mandela Effect discussion. Stan Berenstain recalled a childhood teacher insisting that the surname should be “Bernstein.”<sup><a href="#note-12" aria-label="Research note 12">12</a></sup>

The first Bears book, *The Big Honey Hunt*, appeared in 1962, and the family's own publishing history consistently uses Berenstain.<sup><a href="#note-13" aria-label="Research note 13">13</a></sup>

That does not mean every artifact bearing “Berenstein” is fake. Misprints, catalog errors, unofficial merchandise, and local reproductions can be real physical objects.

But a real misspelling is not the same thing as evidence that the canonical title changed.

The case is useful because it demonstrates how **residue can be genuine and still preserve the error rather than the original**.

An old newspaper can be authentic while its spelling is wrong.

A VHS label can be physically old while still being a production mistake.

A memory can therefore correspond to a real object without establishing that the object was the official source.

That is why provenance outranks mere age.

## A hierarchy for “residue”

The word *residue* is often used as though all surviving anomalies deserve equal evidentiary weight.

They do not.

A useful hierarchy looks something like this:

**Tier 1 — Primary production or institutional record**  
Original artwork, production masters, trademark specimens, publisher files, broadcast masters, official style guides, dated institutional records.

**Tier 2 — Authenticated authorized derivative**  
Licensed merchandise, official regional packaging, publisher-approved promotional material, documented reissues.

**Tier 3 — Contemporary independent record**  
Newspaper advertisement, retailer catalog, library record, television listing, photograph of a product whose date and context can be authenticated.

**Tier 4 — Uncertain derivative**  
Unverified vintage object, local printing, resale item, fan material, counterfeit, bootleg, classroom handout, unattributed scan.

**Tier 5 — Digital reproduction without provenance**  
Screenshot, reposted image, meme, re-created graphic, edited video, anonymous upload.

A Tier 5 image can be a lead.

It is not equivalent to a production master.

This hierarchy also protects skeptical analysis from becoming lazy. If a strong Tier 1 or Tier 2 artifact contradicts the current canonical account, it should not be dismissed because “memory is unreliable.” The archive has to be revised.

## What the experiments do not test

The Visual Mandela Effect studies measured human responses to images.

They did not test:

- alternate universes;
- timeline merging;
- retrocausal edits;
- simulation administrators;
- CERN-induced history changes;
- physical replacement of archived objects;
- or selective transfer of autobiographical memory between branches of reality.

No controlled archive was observed changing.

No preregistered altered-history model identified a future target, predicted the change in advance, and outperformed ordinary memory or exposure models.

No participant produced independently verified historical information that could not be accounted for within the surviving record.

This does not mean extraordinary ideas are prohibited.

It means they have to become **models that can lose**.

## Many-worlds is real physics; cross-branch memory is an extra claim

The Many-Worlds Interpretation of quantum mechanics is a legitimate interpretation of quantum theory with a serious scientific and philosophical literature. Its modern forms descend from Hugh Everett's work and attempt to explain quantum measurement without collapse by treating different outcomes as occurring in different branches or worlds.<sup><a href="#note-14" aria-label="Research note 14">14</a></sup>

That does not make every popular parallel-universe story a consequence of Many-Worlds.

The step from “the formalism can be interpreted as branching worlds” to “a person can move into another branch while retaining memories from the first” is an additional hypothesis.

The Mandela Effect literature reviewed here does not supply a physical mechanism for that transfer.

Nor does the Many-Worlds framework, by itself, predict that cultural artifacts would change while a selected subset of observers retained contradictory autobiographical memories.

Invoking a scientific interpretation is not the same as deriving a testable prediction from it.

## CERN: chronology is not mechanism

CERN is another example of a real scientific institution becoming attached to a claim that needs a causal bridge.

The Large Hadron Collider circulated its first full beam on September 10, 2008.<sup><a href="#note-15" aria-label="Research note 15">15</a></sup> After the 2008 technical incident and shutdown, the LHC returned to operation in November 2009.<sup><a href="#note-16" aria-label="Research note 16">16</a></sup> CERN announced observation of a particle consistent with the Higgs boson on July 4, 2012.<sup><a href="#note-17" aria-label="Research note 17">17</a></sup>

The Mandela Effect's creator-reported origin is 2009.

That chronology immediately rules out one common retelling: the 2012 Higgs announcement cannot be the event that created a concept already being discussed earlier.

The fact that LHC operations and the rise of the Mandela Effect overlap in time does not establish causation. A mechanism would have to connect collider physics to retrospective changes in macroscopic cultural records and explain why memory behaves differently from those records.

No checked physics source in this investigation supplies such a mechanism.

If one is proposed, it should be stated quantitatively enough to generate an observation.

## What would count as evidence for altered history?

“Unsupported” should not become a rhetorical way of making a theory impossible to investigate.

A better approach is to specify what would increase confidence **before** searching for the result.

A serious prospective protocol could do the following:

1. **Preselect targets.** Choose logos, texts, maps, media clips, and physical objects before any alleged change.

2. **Create distributed archival anchors.** Store authenticated copies with multiple independent custodians, including offline copies.

3. **Record cryptographic hashes.** Hash digital files and record the hashes in independent, time-stamped systems.

4. **Document physical objects.** Use high-resolution imaging, chain of custody, serial numbers, provenance, and independent witnesses.

5. **Collect uncued memory in advance.** Have participants draw or describe targets before hearing Mandela Effect language or seeing candidate alternatives.

6. **Predefine what a “change” means.** Does it require a changed physical object, changed file, changed checksum, changed institutional archive, changed memory, or a specific combination?

7. **Predefine residue.** State what would count as preserved evidence and what would be rejected as contamination.

8. **Specify timing.** If a trigger such as a collider run, astronomical event, software deployment, or other event is proposed, define the predicted time window in advance.

9. **Require asymmetric predictions.** The extraordinary model must predict something that ordinary memory, fraud, editing, or archival-error models do not equally predict.

10. **Publish negative results.** If nothing happens, that result stays in the record.

There is a hard boundary here.

If a theory says that every physical object, every digital copy, every hash, and every institutional record will always update perfectly—and only selected subjective memories can remain—then external evidence cannot distinguish it from a world in which no change occurred.

That may remain a metaphysical possibility.

It is not presently a discriminating scientific model.

## The evidence environment is getting worse

The Mandela Effect began as an internet-age memory problem.

Generative media is turning it into a provenance problem.

A preregistered experiment with 200 participants found that exposure to AI-edited images and generated videos increased false recollection, with the strongest condition producing substantially more false memories than unedited controls.<sup><a href="#note-18" aria-label="Research note 18">18</a></sup> Importantly, that work is no longer only a preprint: a final version was published in the proceedings of CHI 2025.

A separate 2024 study of simulated witness interviews reported that an LLM-based conversational interviewer using misleading questions produced more false memories than control conditions, with effects persisting at follow-up; that work remains a research-stage publication and should be described accordingly.<sup><a href="#note-19" aria-label="Research note 19">19</a></sup>

Neither study explains the older Mandela Effect.

They change the future evidence environment.

A synthetic “vintage” logo can now be produced in seconds. Once reposted without provenance, it can become part of people's exposure history. A generated commercial can be mistaken for an old broadcast. Search systems can surface the synthetic version beside authentic archives. Years later, researchers may encounter a population whose memories were genuinely influenced by material that did not exist when the original controversy began.

From this point forward, a Mandela investigation should ask not only **where did this artifact come from?** but **when was this exact file first known to exist?**

Hashes, content credentials, archived captures, and pre-AI provenance will become increasingly important.

## What the evidence supports

Several conclusions are stronger now than they were when the Mandela Effect was mainly an internet anecdote.

**Shared, specific visual-memory convergence is real.**  
The 2022 experiments demonstrated it for selected stimuli, and the 2024 preregistered replication recovered the central effect in a larger independent sample.<sup><a href="#note-4" aria-label="Research note 4">4</a></sup><sup><a href="#note-5" aria-label="Research note 5">5</a></sup>

**The original statistical analysis was not fully sound.**  
The replication identified an inappropriate chi-square analysis and other clarity/reproducibility problems. Correcting them altered part of the original result without removing the main pattern.<sup><a href="#note-5" aria-label="Research note 5">5</a></sup>

**One universal cognitive mechanism has not been established.**  
Gist, schema, source monitoring, exposure, attention, social transmission, and stimulus-specific properties are all plausible contributors. None has been shown to explain every case.

**Specific popular examples require archive work.**  
A replicated false choice does not prove that no historical variant ever existed.

**Some residue can be authentic without proving the remembered canonical version.**  
A genuine old misprint, counterfeit, derivative object, or database classification can preserve an error.

**The original Nelson Mandela death claim conflicts with an extensive historical record.**  
The source of the detailed remembered funeral narrative remains unidentified.<sup><a href="#note-3" aria-label="Research note 3">3</a></sup>

**Many-Worlds quantum mechanics does not, by itself, establish cross-branch memory transfer.**  
That is a separate hypothesis requiring its own mechanism and predictions.<sup><a href="#note-14" aria-label="Research note 14">14</a></sup>

**No checked evidence presently demonstrates physical alteration of historical reality.**  
That conclusion is evidence-based, not a declaration that such a thing is philosophically impossible.

## Evidence status

- Shared specific visual-memory errors: **Documented and independently replicated**
- Original 2022 analysis: **Substantively supported, with documented statistical corrections**
- Free-drawing production of characteristic errors: **Supported; less extensively replicated**
- One universal explanation: **Unknown / unresolved**
- Prior exposure ruled out for every case: **Not established**
- Every “residue” artifact explained: **Not established**
- Mandela's historical survival through 1990 and presidency in 1994: **Documented**
- Source of the remembered 1980s funeral narrative: **Unknown**
- Many-Worlds as a quantum interpretation: **Documented**
- Cross-branch memory transfer as an explanation of the Mandela Effect: **Unverified**
- CERN mechanism for retrospective cultural change: **Unsupported by checked evidence**
- Physical timeline alteration: **Not demonstrated**

## What should be investigated next

The best next paper is not “50 Mandela Effects That Will Blow Your Mind.”

It is a single-case forensic audit.

Fruit of the Loom is the strongest candidate because it contains all the necessary layers: a replicated visual-memory effect, a precise remembered object, a disputed government-record clue, a large body of alleged physical residue, and decades of potential merchandise to reconstruct.

The archive should be frozen first.

Only then should a new memory experiment begin.

Participants should be recruited without using the phrase *Mandela Effect*. They should be asked to draw and describe the logo without seeing alternatives. Their relevant exposure history should be recorded. The historical archive and the memory sample should be analyzed separately before they are compared.

That design could answer a much sharper question:

> **Can the cornucopia memory be traced to a real historical visual pathway, or does the same detail arise independently even among people for whom no cornucopia-bearing exposure can be documented?**

The answer could strengthen an ordinary explanation, reveal a forgotten authorized variant, expose a derivative source, or create a genuinely new puzzle.

Any of those outcomes would be more valuable than deciding in advance what the phenomenon is allowed to be.

The deepest lesson of the Mandela Effect is not that memory cannot be trusted.

It is that **confidence, agreement, and physical evidence are three different things**.

Sometimes people are confidently wrong.

Sometimes they are wrong in the same way.

Sometimes the object they remember really did exist, but not in the role they assigned to it.

And sometimes the archive is incomplete.

A serious investigation has to be able to tell those possibilities apart.
